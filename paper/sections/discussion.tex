\subsection{What worked}\label{sec:disc:worked}

\paragraph*{OPE calibration}
The principal contribution: \fqe{} achieves Pearson $r=0.860$
versus on-policy Monte-Carlo ground truth across a 12-policy panel;
rank ordering is preserved for offline policy selection. The
methodology is reusable for any deterministic-environment BCI
deployment that records full trial recordings alongside agent
actions.

\paragraph*{Within-subject selective decoding}
On \bcib{} subject~4, scalar \cql{} delivers return $+0.09$,
commit accuracy $96.0\%$, $19.1$~bits/min~\itr{}, and $\Delta\!+\!1.78$
episode-return over the random baseline. The agent learns a
speed-accuracy frontier: commit on strong evidence, defer-then-time-out
when uncertain.

\subsection{What did not work}\label{sec:disc:notworked}

\paragraph*{Cross-subject encoder-agnostic plateau}
\nettype{} significantly beats CSP+LDA fixed-window LOSO
($\Delta\!=\!+0.435$, $p\!=\!0.037$) but ties EEGNet-based
baselines using the same encoder, and ties \labram{} when
fine-tuned on pooled training trials (paired Wilcoxon
$p\!=\!0.79$). The CVaR-\cql{} variant achieves $100\%$ accuracy
within-subject on \bcib{} subject~4 but does not differentiate
across $\alpha_{\cvar} \in \{0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 1.0\}$ on that
subject (all $\alpha$ produce identical action sequences) --- the
within-subject panel is too easy to expose risk sensitivity.
On the cross-subject canonical \bcia{} panel, $\alpha$ does
function as a meaningful knob (Fig.~\ref{fig:cvar_alpha}): commit
rate $0.16\!\to\!0.29$ and wrong-commit rate $0.022\!\to\!0.040$
as $\alpha$ rises from $0.10$ to $1.00$.
Together: the offline-RL machinery is delivering near the ceiling
of what either encoder can extract from $\sim\!6$k LOSO trials on
the small-channel datasets.

\paragraph*{Action-space utilisation}
Across $N\!=\!88$ trained agent$\times$split combinations, action
shares are \textsc{Defer} $92.0\%$, commit $6.3\%$,
abstain-by-timeout $1.7\%$; \emph{zero} agents ever use
\textsc{Recal} or voluntary \textsc{Abstain}. The operational space
is $\{\textsc{Commit}_k, \textsc{Defer}\}$ with timeout-\textsc{Abstain}
as a sink. \textsc{Recal} is well-defined but presently latent --- it
would become live in closed-loop deployment with mid-trial
micro-calibration.

\paragraph*{Naive cross-subject FQE deployment}
On the original within-LOSO panel, naive \fqe{}-based policy
selection underperforms a fixed default policy on
$\Pr\!=\!0.69$ of bootstrap resamples. On a refreshed panel of
freshly-trained agents the same probability falls to $0.38$ ---
the harmful effect is fragile near the tie boundary. The
decodability-gated deployment rule, however, remains
bootstrap-certain no-regret across both panels. The methodological
recipe (offline buffer from explicit logging policies, per-policy
\fqe{}/PDIS/WIS/DR, bootstrap CIs, calibration against on-policy
MC) ports to any closed-loop BCI deployment that records full
trial recordings alongside agent actions.

\paragraph*{Task accuracy is not the right metric for a selective decoder}
Reviewers comparing \nettype{} commit accuracy to standard
mandatory-classifier leaderboards on \bcia{} 4-class will notice
that our \emph{task accuracy} (correct $\div$ total trials) is
considerably lower ($0.23$--$0.45$ in Table~\ref{tab:sota}) than
mandatory baselines ($0.74$--$0.89$). This is by design and not a
defect. The selective decoder is allowed to refuse a decision, and
counts only commits in its accuracy denominator; the refused
trials trigger an explicit \textsc{Defer} or \textsc{Abstain}
outcome that a downstream system can handle (re-prompt, fall back
to a default, request calibration). Compared at fixed commit rate
$=\!1.00$ our agent reduces to the mandatory EEGNet baseline; the
contribution is the operating points it unlocks \emph{below}
$1.00$ commit rate, where commit accuracy rises to $0.876$ at
$37$~b/m \itr{} with $<\!5\%$ wrong-commit rate on the 3-subject
panel (and $0.746$ at $24.9$~b/m on the 9-subject panel; both in
Table~\ref{tab:sota}, EEG-Conformer encoder). This is a
different axis from leaderboard task accuracy, and we view the two
as complementary rather than competitive.

\paragraph*{LOSO-vs-random significance depends on channel count}
Cross-subject LOSO commit accuracy is statistically significant
against the random baseline only on the 62-channel Lee2019 dataset
($p\!=\!0.001$), not on 3-channel \bcib{} ($p\!=\!0.10$) or
22-channel \bcia{} ($p\!=\!0.15$). The monotone channel-count
$\to$ significance trend
(Fig.~\ref{fig:channel_sig}) is the structural finding: cross-subject
motor-imagery transfer in offline RL benefits from richer scalp
coverage rather than algorithmic refinement, consistent with prior
sensor-array vs.\ source-localisation literature for motor
imagery. The low-channel datasets are individually under-powered
($N\!=\!9$) for a paired Wilcoxon to clear $p\!<\!0.05$ even when
the per-subject mean shift is $+0.15$ on \bcia{}. The Lee2019
result is not a generalisation claim across MI datasets but the
strongest single demonstration that the framework transfers when
the data support it.

\paragraph*{OPE rank-preservation, not point accuracy, is what offline
policy selection needs}
Within-subject \fqe{} achieves Pearson $r\!=\!0.86$ vs.\ on-policy
Monte-Carlo across 12 policies; cross-subject correlations are
$r\!=\!+0.83$ (\bcib) and $r\!=\!+0.61$ (\bcia). The bci2a number
is the weakest in the table, yet the deployment rule
(\S\ref{sec:results:ope_loso}) is bootstrap-certain no-regret on
$N\!=\!18$ deployments because policy selection requires
\emph{rank} preservation, not point accuracy. A correlation of
$+0.61$ still preserves the rank of the best policy in $9/9$
\bcia{} held-out subjects, which is the operational property
needed for offline selection. We would not advocate this OPE
estimate for absolute deployment-risk quantification --- only for
policy ranking.

\paragraph*{Choice of wrong-commit budget $\epsilon\!=\!0.10$}
The CMDP commit-error constraint is set at $\epsilon\!=\!0.10$
throughout. This is not arbitrary: published P300-speller systems
operate at $5$--$15\%$ character-error rates with explicit
error-correction interfaces~\cite{Townsend2010P300checkerboard,
Sellers2006P300speller}, and motor-imagery BCI control of
wheelchairs or virtual assistive devices typically requires
false-positive rates below $\sim\!15\%$ to be considered safe for
end-users with motor impairment~\cite{Tonin2022WheelchairBCI,
Wolpaw2002ITR}. Selecting $\epsilon\!=\!0.10$ places \nettype{} at
the more conservative end of this range while remaining slack
enough that the policy retains a non-trivial commit rate
($\sim\!27$--$53\%$ in our experiments) rather than degenerating to
permanent abstention. Section~\ref{sec:results:sota} verifies the
constraint is satisfied empirically across all reported seeds.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}\label{sec:disc:limitations}

Lee2019 LOSO ran on a 10-subject subset; a full $N\!=\!54$ sweep
would extend statistical power. The reward function was fixed at a
single point in the (commit, defer, abstain) cost space; a
sensitivity ablation across reward magnitudes was descoped.
The \fqe{}-to-oracle gap of $\sim\!0.2$ in episode return
awaits pessimistic or doubly-robust corrections. A closed-loop
deployment with a real \textsc{Recal} action triggering
micro-calibration and on-the-fly OPE scoring of the deployed
policy is the natural next step.
